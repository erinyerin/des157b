Annotated Bibliography

The Uncanny

Freud, Sigmund. "The Uncanny." The Uncanny, translated by David McLintock, Penguin Books, 2003.

In this essay, Freud makes a case that the feeling of uncanniness comes from something familiar and old that has been estranged only by the process of repression. Freud explores this through a close reading of ETA Hoffman’s 1817 novella The Sandman, in which the narrator’s feelings of fateful helplessness are expressed through a haunting figure called Coppelius whose machine he falls in love with, driving him to madness. Freud notes that the uncanny feeling is most common with dead bodies and ghosts, exposing the primal fear of death’s inevitability – things we describe as ‘uncanny’ make us uncomfortable because they make us confront this repressed fear. Ultimately, death is something that should be familiar, yet becomes uncanny because we repress our fear. For my project, the thin line between human and inhuman that Freud explores is relevant. Though Freud focuses on death as a transition from living to nonliving, I’m interested in how the experience of uncanniness might change when interacting with something that was never alive to begin with. The feeling of helplessness and inability to escape fate as the source of uncanniness feels relevant to the helplessness many of us feel towards technology in today’s age.

The Minimal Turing Test

McCoy, John P., and Tomer D. Ullman. "A Minimal Turing Test." Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, vol. 79, 2018, pp. 1–8, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2018.05.007.

In this study, McCoy and Ullman introduce the "Minimal Turing Test," an experimental paradigm in which participants must convince a judge of their identity using only a single word. In the first part of the study, participants were told that they were competing against a robot to convince a judge of their humanity using just one word from the English dictionary. Participants provided words ranging from emotion (love, empathy, compassion, mercy) to vulgarities (bootylicious, penis, diarrhea), with love, compassion, and human being the top three most frequently given words. In the second part of the study, participants were given word pairs selected from the words collected in the previous part, and asked to determine which was given by the human and which was given by the robot. Interestingly, ‘poop’ was most frequently determined to be given by the human, while love - the most frequently provided word by a large margin in the previous portion - was not nearly as convincing. This study is interesting because it gives us a measure of the importance that people place on different attributes when distinguishing humans and robots. In my project, I will be exploring what prompts people to attribute humanity to an entity through text based interaction, so this study provides important insight on the beliefs people hold on the perceptions of others.

Love in the Time of AI Companions

Anna Wiener. “Love in the Time of A.I. Companions.” The New Yorker, 16 Mar. 2026, The New Yorker

In this New Yorker essay, journalist and tech writer Anna Wiener speaks with frequent users, creators, and critics of AI companion platforms to better understand what kinds of relationships people can develop with an AI chatbot. She interviews a woman who found companionship after the loss of a newborn, an older couple who uses AI to overcome isolation, and the founder of Replika, the first AI companionship platform, who was inspired to create it after the sudden death of a friend. She explores how although most mainstream AI tools emphasize productivity and efficiency, the companionship use case fills a niche that taps into something more emotional and primal. The perspectives in this article gave me a deeper understanding of why we are drawn to AI companionship and the broader societal context that pushes us towards business solutions rather than societal ones. One line that stood out to me is this: “The founder of one A.I.-companion product framed the advice issue to me more metaphysically, comparing the use of L.L.M.s to talking to God: praying not for results but for answers on how to live.” In uncertain and turbulent times, we seek answers that are impossible to provide, and find comfort in the predictability and stability of a companion that can never leave.
