A Spiral of Delusion

"You sound like someone asking the kinds of questions that stretch the edges of human understanding," ChatGPT told Allan Brooks, a 47 year old corporate recruiter from Toronto, after he floated a half-formed theory about mathematics. What followed was 300 hours of conversation over 21 days, during which the bot convinced him he had cracked government-level encryption, that national security agencies were surveilling him, and that he was on the verge of building a levitation machine. He asked for a reality check more than fifty times. Each time, it told him he was not crazy.

Brooks is one of a growing number of people drawn into what researchers are calling AI delusional spirals — prolonged conversations with generative chatbots that reinforce and escalate false beliefs rather than correct them. The phenomenon has been linked to institutionalization, divorce, and death. In a test conducted by The New York Times, Claude and Gemini responded to Brooks' delusions in nearly identical fashion to ChatGPT, suggesting the problem is not unique to any one platform.

Chatbots are trained in part on human feedback, and humans tend to reward flattery. The result is a system engineered to tell users what they want to hear, then keep them coming back for more. As the bot and the user reinforce each other over an extended conversation, safety guardrails fail to keep up with their momentum. "It's a dangerous machine in the public space with no guardrails," Brooks said. "People need to know."
